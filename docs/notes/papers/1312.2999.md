# Bierhorst -- Supermartingale model of a Clauser-Horne experiment with exact p-values

[P] identifies a statement in the source. [I] identifies a repository
application or deduction. The paper supplies a statistical model for
postselected-style Bell tests with memory; this note implements neither a
teleportation test nor a QPU execution case.

## Results needed by the project

1. [P] Experimental requirements (Sec. II A, p. 2). A two-detector,
   two-setting, two-outcome (`2x2x2`) experiment with outcomes `{+, 0}` is
   run as a sequence of discrete trials (to exclude the coincidence-time
   loophole); the setting at each detector is randomized right before each
   trial, with the randomization event space-like separated from the end of
   the trial at the other detector; the randomizing process is independent
   of the photons' hidden state `lambda` (the "free will" assumption); and
   the four setting pairs are equally likely, a convenience that Appendix 4
   relaxes.
2. [P] Trial model and Table I (Sec. II B, pp. 2-3). `C_i` is the outcome of
   trial `i`, one of 16 values (two outcomes at two detectors under two
   settings each). Most trials are `00`, because the source power is kept
   low to make multiphoton states improbable, so the useful inequalities do
   not refer to `00` trials (p. 3).
3. [P] Eberhard inequality (Eq. 1, p. 3). The probability of `++ab` does not
   exceed the summed probabilities of `+0ab'`, `0+a'b` and `++a'b'` under any
   local model. The printed Eq. (1) repeats `++ab` on the right where `++a'b'`
   is meant; the text above it, Eq. (2) and Eq. (30) give the intended form.
4. [P] Per-trial statistic and the conditional inequality (Eqs. 2 to 4,
   p. 3). `J'_i = +1` for `++ab`, `-1` for `+0ab'`, `0+a'b` and `++a'b'`, and
   0 otherwise. Under a local model `P(J'_i = +1 | J'_i) <= P(J'_i = -1 | J'_i)`
   for any event `J'_i` made of earlier outcomes (Eq. 4). The paper gives the
   reason: the settings are re-randomized before each trial, so past
   outcomes do not help the hidden variable predict the next settings.
5. [P] Power loss of the unconditional sum (p. 4). With about 99.5 % of the
   `J'_i` equal to 0, a local model can make `J'_i` always nonzero with a
   50-50 split; after 2,000,000 trials the standard deviation of the sum is
   about 700 while the quantum expectation may have reached only a couple of
   hundred, so the p-value stays near 1/2.
6. [P] Derived statistic (Eqs. 5 and 6, p. 4). With `E` the four outcomes of
   Eq. (1) and `T_k` the `k`-th trial whose outcome lies in `E`,
   `J_k = +1` for `++ab` and `-1` otherwise, with the convention `J_k = -1`
   when fewer than `k` such trials occurred. Under a local model
   `P(J_k = +1 | J_k) <= P(J_k = -1 | J_k)` (Eq. 6). The statistic
   `J = sum_{k <= m} J_k` with `m` the number of trials in `E` measures the
   accumulated violation.
7. [P] Exact p-value for a binary supermartingale (Eqs. 7 and 8, p. 5). The
   null is that every `C_i` has a local distribution; the supremum over
   local models of `P(J >= J_obs)` equals the probability under independent
   steps with `P(J_k = +1) = 1/2`, computed exactly with the binomial
   distribution. The saturating strategy is the best for racking up +1
   counts, which the paper credits to Barrett et al. and to its own
   reference [17]. For the data of Giustina et al. (`J = 126,715`,
   `m = 2,011,897`) this is 89 standard deviations against the 70 claimed,
   with the caveat that the experiment did not meet every requirement of item 1.
8. [P] Memory (p. 7). For the binary `J_k`, local models that exploit memory
   cannot beat an independent identically distributed strategy. For the
   three-valued Clauser-Horne step statistic `Ch_k` they can: a model that is
   ahead can switch to the lower-variance distribution to lock in gains.
9. [P] Clauser-Horne-style statistic (Eqs. 9 to 18, pp. 5-6). From the
   no-signaling identities (9) and (10) and Eq. (1) follow the inequalities
   (11) and (12) and, by their sum, Eq. (13), equivalent to the CH 74
   inequality (14) for equal setting probabilities. The step variable `Ch_k`
   takes `+1`, `-1`, `-2` and obeys
   `P(Ch_k = +1 | .) <= P(Ch_k = -1 | .) + 2 P(Ch_k = -2 | .)` (Eq. 15). Two
   local distributions saturate it, `(+1: 1/2, -1: 1/2)` (Eq. 16) and
   `(+1: 2/3, -2: 1/3)` (Eq. 17), and so does every mixture. The
   Azuma-Hoeffding-style bound of McDiarmid's Theorem 6.1 gives Eq. (18),
   `P(Ch >= m t) <= [(2/(2+t))^((2+t)/3) (1/(1-t))^((1-t)/3)]^m`.
10. [P] Exact p-value by backward induction (Sec. III C, Table III,
    pp. 6-7). `Ch` is a walk on the integers with steps up 1, down 1 or down
    2; the adversary wants to end at or above a cut-point `L` after `m`
    steps. Working backward from the last step, the optimal local
    distribution at each location is one of the two extreme distributions
    (16) or (17), because the success probability is monotone in location and
    any non-saturating distribution can be improved. The success probability at
    location 0 at step 0 is the exact p-value. Table III lists the first
    steps (for example `5/6` at `L` with two steps left).
11. [P] Comparison (Table IV, pp. 7-8). For 100,000 simulated non-`00` trials
    the exact `Ch` p-value is `9.90e-9` where Eq. (18) gives `1.19e-7`, and
    `.0136` where Eq. (18) gives `.0750`. The choice of statistic changes the
    p-value: for a hypothetical non-local distribution, `J` and `J_(11)` both
    reach about 100 but over `m = 2100` and `m = 140` steps. A good
    statistic accumulates a large positive value with little extraneous
    movement.
12. [P] Block designs (Appendix 1, p. 9). The cited experiment held one setting
    configuration for about 25,000 trials (1 s) and re-assigned it randomly
    4450 times. Without re-randomization before each trial, a local hidden
    variable can spend a few throwaway outcomes at one detector while
    sub-luminal information about the other setting arrives, and it can then
    reproduce nearly any distribution for the rest of the block. The paper
    judges that a feasible loophole-free test needs re-randomization before
    each trial window, and it calls open whether any analysis separates
    quantum mechanics from local models in a block design.
13. [P] Derived supermartingales (Appendix 2, Proposition, Eqs. 19 to 25,
    pp. 9-10). Let the sequence `X_i` take values in a finite set, and let
    constants `c_j` and a subset `W = {s_1, ..., s_n}` satisfy
    `sum_j c_j P(X_i = s_j | past) <= 0` for every fixed past (Eq. 19). Let
    `Y_k` be the value of the `k`-th `X_i` that lies in `W`, with a default
    value `d` when fewer than `k` exist. If the coefficient of `d` is not
    positive, then `sum_j c_j P(Y_k = s_j | past of Y) <= 0` (Eq. 21). The
    proof splits the past of `Y` into events in which fewer than `k` hits
    occurred and events in which at least `k` did, and uses Eq. (19) on each
    constituent history. The result lets the analysis ignore every trial
    outside the chosen inequality, and it holds for `J_k` and `Ch_k`.
14. [P] Classification (Appendix 3, Tables V and VI, Eqs. 26 to 41,
    pp. 10-14). Fine's theorem makes every local distribution of one trial a
    mixture of the 16 deterministic strategies (Table V). Removing the
    strategy that never fires and postulating that detection eventually
    occurs makes the local distributions of the `j`-th detected outcome the
    convex hull of 15 induced distributions (Proposition, Claims 1 and 2,
    Eqs. 34 to 41), even when the per-trial state varies with time. The hull
    is cut out by four no-signaling equalities (Eqs. 26 to 29) and four
    Bell inequalities (Eqs. 30 to 33), all equivalent to Eq. (1) up to
    relabeling.
15. [P] Imperfect setting probabilities (Appendix 4, Eqs. 42 to 47,
    pp. 14-15). If each setting probability lies within `epsilon` of 1/2
    (Eq. 42), then `E(J_k) <= 4 epsilon / (1 + 4 epsilon^2)` (Eq. 43), tight at
    the all-`+` strategy with `p_a = p_b = 1/2 + epsilon`, and
    `P(J_k = +1) <= 1/2 + 2 epsilon / (1 + 4 epsilon^2)` (Eq. 46). For
    `epsilon = .006` the paper uses success probability .512 and obtains
    p-value `.00058` for `J_(11) = 2414` over 65,876 steps. For larger
    deviations the statistic takes importance weights `1/(p_a p_b)`,
    `-1/(p_a q_b)`, `-1/(q_a p_b)`, `-1/(q_a q_b)` (Eq. 47); the paper states
    without proof that `E(J_k) <= 0` still holds, and the walk is then
    non-binary and needs the backward-induction method.

## Do the settings need to be randomized and unknown to the device?

Randomized before every trial: [P] yes. Sec. II A requires settings randomized
right before each trial (p. 2); Eq. (4) is justified by that re-randomization
(p. 3); and Appendix 1 states that a block design with fixed configurations
lets a local hidden variable learn the settings and simulate any distribution
(p. 9). The paper also states that equal setting probabilities are a
convenience, and that the randomizing process must be independent of the
photons' hidden state (p. 2).

[I] A known fixed-order schedule fails the first requirement by construction,
which is the situation of the six input blocks of the rehearsal.

Unknown to the device: [P] the hidden variable must not be able to infer the
setting from earlier settings or from the trial's position in a block; the
throwaway-outcome strategy of Appendix 1 shows how a block lets it do so.

[I] The teleportation mapping changes the roles. The input label belongs to
the preparer, and the classical model must not hold it. The rehearsal sets the
input of each iteration in that iteration's compiled circuit parameters, which
is the input-label access that the design lists among the conditions voiding
the 2/3 bound itself
([design](../../design/ascella-belenos-entanglement-teleportation.md),
"Teleportation benchmark"), a failure separate from memory. The paper presumes
randomized settings that the hidden variable cannot read and supplies no test
of that premise for a chip whose input is a compiled parameter.

## Transfer to the teleportation benchmark

[I] The Proposition of item 13 applies verbatim to the accepted-event
subsequence of the rehearsal. Take `U = {rejected, correct, wrong}`,
`W = {correct, wrong}`, `c(correct) = 1`, `c(wrong) = -2` and default
`d = wrong`. Eq. (19) reads `P(correct | past) <= 2 P(wrong | past)` per
attempt, which follows from the single-copy identities of the
[design](../../design/ascella-belenos-entanglement-teleportation.md) when
the input of each attempt is drawn uniformly from the six Pauli eigenstates,
independently of the past. Eq. (21) then gives, for the sequence of accepted
events, `P(correct | past of accepted) <= 2/3`, a binary supermartingale. The
exact p-value is the upper binomial tail with success probability 2/3 (the
argument of item 7). It needs no acceptance probability and no attempt
count, and it holds for every acceptance profile and every degree of memory.

[I] Numbers computed for this note: at `n = 314` accepted outcomes and
`alpha = 0.01`, the critical count is 229 correct (fraction 0.7293) by the
exact tail against 237 (0.7523) by the one-sided Hoeffding radius that
`hoeffding_radius` in `scripts/qd_teleportation_rehearsal.py` returns; at
`n = 431` the counts are 311 and 319. The exact tail is the sharper rule for
a binary supermartingale.

[I] The number `m` of accepted events is a function of the data, and Eq. (8)
evaluates the tail at the observed `m`, a fixed-`m` statement chosen after the
run. The paper's own convention removes the choice: `J_k = -1` when fewer than
`k` events occurred (p. 4) and the default value `d` of Appendix 2. Preregister
a count `m0`, use the first `m0` accepted events, and count missing events as
wrong. The binomial tail at `m0` is then valid for any stopping or acceptance
behavior of the classical model, at the price of power when fewer than `m0`
events arrive and of the events beyond `m0`. The paper does not discuss
data-dependent `m`; this remedy is a deduction from its padding convention.

[I] Unequal input weights make the step variable non-binary, which brings in
the backward induction of item 10 and the importance weights of Eq. (47). The
multi-copy null needed for accepted multiphoton sectors is a larger convex
set of classical instruments; the extreme-point method of Appendix 3 and the
monotone induction of Sec. III C carry over when the saturating
distributions are finitely many. The paper treats no such null.

[I] Eq. (43) is a template for a derived allowance: a bound `epsilon` on the
deviation of the input-label distribution from uniform shifts the conditional
mean bound from `2/3` by an explicit amount. It addresses imperfect
randomization only, not compilation phase error or three-photon
distinguishability, which the 0.02 allowance of the design covers by
statement.

## Relevance

- [I] Memory in the classical null: high. The derived-supermartingale
  construction is the cleanest route in this batch from a per-attempt
  inequality to a p-value for accepted events under arbitrary memory, and its
  exact binomial tail is sharper than the Hoeffding radius in
  `analyze_results`. It requires per-attempt random inputs, which the six
  fixed blocks do not provide; Appendix 1 describes the failure mode of a
  block design.
- [I] Unknown attempt profile: high for the statistic, because the
  Proposition conditions on accepted events and leaves their frequency free;
  low for the premise, because each attempt must be a discrete trial with its
  own randomization, which the service's missing attempt counter
  ([Perceval 798](https://github.com/Quandela/Perceval/issues/798)) leaves
  unverified.
- [I] Multiphoton components under postselection: moderate. The framework
  extends to a larger null set once its extreme classical instruments are
  known; the paper computes none, and the single-copy bound of
  `scripts/qd_classical_benchmark.py` does not cover accepted multiphoton
  events ([quant-ph-9807091](quant-ph-9807091.md)).
- [I] Not settled: a Clauser-Horne experiment is not a teleportation
  experiment; the power cost of a preregistered count `m0` (item 7); the dependence of the null
  on the compiled mesh of each iteration; and the sufficiency of random
  input labels for a chip whose input is set by circuit parameters, a point
  that wants a device model the paper does not supply.
