## Setting and conventions (Sections 1 and 2, pp. 129-130)

- [P] Introduction (pp. 129-130): the standard basis of an ideal of `Q[x]` is computed modulo one prime or several primes `p` (lifting by Hensel lifting after Winkler for one prime and by the Chinese remainder theorem after Sasaki and Takeshima for several, then Farey fractions to recover rational coefficients); this paper follows the Chinese remainder route.
  After the lifting two problems remain: that the lift is a standard basis in characteristic zero and that it generates the ideal one started with. For Groebner bases (a global monomial ordering) and homogeneous ideals the paper points to Arnold for a reasonable solution, and states that the same method also works for standard bases with respect to local orderings (p. 129).
  Page 130 states that for mixed orderings, or global orderings with non-homogeneous ideals, the same methods "just" give a standard basis generating an ideal containing the starting ideal, which the paper regards as sufficient for experiments and insufficient for proofs.
- [P] Section 2 (p. 130): `I` is an ideal of `Q[x]`, `x = (x_1, ..., x_n)`, and `>` a monomial ordering; `I_0 = I intersect Z[x]` and `I_p` is printed as `I_0 Z/p[x]` for a prime `p`, the ideal of `F_p[x]` generated by the reductions of `I_0`. `L(I)` and `L(I_p)` are the minimal generating sets of leading monomials. The prime `p` is lucky for `I` with respect to `>` if `L(I) = L(I_p)`.
  The paper does not define "local ordering" and cites Greuel and Pfister for the definitions and properties of standard bases (footnote, p. 130).
- [P] Remark 2.1 (p. 130): for monic polynomials `g_1, ..., g_s` of `Q[x]` forming a standard basis of `I Q[x]_>`, with standard representations `u_ij spoly(g_i, g_j) = sum_k h_ijk g_k` and `u_ij` a unit of `Q[x]_>`, every prime that divides no denominator of the coefficients of the `g_i`, `u_ij` and `h_ijk` is lucky.

## Hilbert-function criteria for lucky primes and for equality of ideals (Section 2, pp. 130-132)

- [P] Proposition 2.2 (p. 130), under the standing assumption that either `I` is homogeneous or `>` is a local ordering: with `H_I` and `H_(I_p)` the Hilbert function (`I` homogeneous) or the Hilbert-Samuel function (local ordering) of `Q[x]_> / I Q[x]_>` and `F_p[x]_> / I_p F_p[x]_>`, `H_I(n) <= H_(I_p)(n)` for all `n`.
  A second statement of the proposition: if `H_I = H_(I_p)`, with `L(I) = {f_1, ..., f_s}` and `L(I_p) = {m_1, ..., m_k}` listed in increasing order and `f_i = m_i` for `i < l` (`1 <= l <= min{s, k}`), then `m_l <= f_l`. The paper states that the proof "is not difficult" and gives it by reference to Arnold for the global case; no proof of the local case is printed.
- [P] Corollary 2.3 (pp. 130-131): with the assumptions of Proposition 2.2, let `J` be an ideal such that (1) `I` lies in `J`, (2) in case of a non-local ordering `J` is homogeneous, and (3) `H_(I_p) = H_J` for some prime `p`; then `I = J`. The proof (p. 131) is the chain `H_(I_p)(n) = H_J(n) <= H_I(n) <= H_(I_p)(n)`.
  No luckiness of `p` is required, and the criterion compares Hilbert functions, not leading monomials.
- [P] Corollary 2.4 (p. 131): for primes `p` and `q`, the prime `q` is not lucky if either (1) `H_(I_p)(n) = H_(I_q)(n)` for `n < n_0` and `H_(I_p)(n_0) < H_(I_q)(n_0)`, or (2) `H_I = H_(I_p)` and the sorted lists of leading monomials `L(I_p) = {f_1, ..., f_s}` and `L(I_q) = {m_1, ..., m_k}` agree below an index `l` with `m_l < f_l`.
  The proof says that a lucky `q` would give `L(I) = L(I_q)`, hence `H_I = H_(I_q)`, which Proposition 2.2 excludes.
- [P] The procedure `deleteUnluckyPrimes(T, L)` (pp. 131-132, Singular code): for a list `T` of standard bases and a list `L` of primes it selects among the leading ideals the one with the smallest first Hilbert series (compared as integer vectors) and, among equal Hilbert series, the one with the larger leading monomial at the first index of difference (the direction that Proposition 2.2 gives for the leading ideal of a lucky prime), deletes every prime whose leading ideal differs from the selected one, and returns the lists and the selected leading ideal.
  Remark 2.5 (p. 132): the first Hilbert series is `Q(t) / (1 - t)^n` with an integer polynomial `Q`; comparing the coefficient vectors of two numerators lexicographically detects, by the first index of difference, the unlucky prime of Corollary 2.4(1), and the first Hilbert series is computed in Singular by `hilb(I, 1)`.

## The modular algorithm (Section 2, pp. 132-135)

- [P] Remark 2.5 and the text after it (pp. 132-133): for lucky primes `p_1, ..., p_r`, the standard bases `G_(p_i)` of `I_(p_i)` are taken minimal, monic, uniquely determined by a fixed algorithm, and sorted by increasing leading monomial; the footnote (p. 132) adds that for a global ordering one may take the reduced Groebner basis, whereas for a local ordering reduced standard bases exist only for zero-dimensional ideals,
  which is why an algorithm is fixed. The Chinese remainder theorem and Farey fractions give `G = {f_1, ..., f_l}` with `f_i` monic in `Q[x]`, an integer `d` prime to every `p_k` with `d f_i` in `Z[x]`, and `d f_i` reducing modulo `p_k` to `(d mod p_k)` times `f_i^(p_k)` (p. 133).
- [P] Proposition 2.6 (p. 133): for a random choice of `p_1, ..., p_r` with `r` big enough, `G` is a standard basis of `I Q[x]_>`. The proof bounds numerators and denominators of the coefficients by `m`, takes the primes with `2 m^2 < p_1 ... p_r` so that the coefficients are `m`-th Farey fractions mapping injectively to the product of the `F_(p_i)`, and concludes that the reductions of the standard representations make `{f_i mod p_k}` a standard basis of `I_(p_k)`, hence equal to `G_(p_k)`.
  Remark 2.7 (p. 133): no efficient bound on numerators and denominators of a minimal standard basis is known in terms of the generators, so the number of primes is found by trial and error; Buchberger's algorithm applied to polynomials that already form a standard basis is usually cheaper than applied to generators that do not.
- [P] Algorithm `modstd (S)` (pp. 133-134): input a finite set `S` in `Z[x]`; output `G` in `Q[x]`, a minimal standard basis of `<S> Q[x]_>` for a fixed ordering. Step (2) collects five random primes and the standard bases `M_p` of `S F_p[x]_>` and deletes unlucky primes; step (3) lifts by the Chinese remainder theorem and Farey fractions to a system `M`; step (4) adds one fresh random prime at a time, deletes unlucky primes, returns to step (2) when more than one prime was deleted and otherwise to the lifting;
  step (5) computes by Buchberger's algorithm a standard basis of `M` and returns to step (1) if it differs from `M`; step (6) reduces `S` with respect to `M` and, if a remainder is nonzero, adds primes again; step (7) returns `M`.
- [P] Remark 2.8 (pp. 134-135): if the ordering is not local, or the ideal generated by `S` is not homogeneous, the test in step (6) implies only `<S> Q[x]_> <= <M> Q[x]_>`, because Corollary 2.3 does not hold in general. To obtain equality one could lift, together with the standard bases `M_p`, the relations that express `<S> F_p[x]_>` as `<M_p> F_p[x]_>` (computed in Singular by `liftstd`); the paper reports that experiments showed this to be much more expensive.
  Footnote (p. 133): the algorithms are implemented in the library `modstd.lib`.

## Examples (Section 3, pp. 135-136)

- [P] Section 3 (pp. 135-136): four examples, all zero-dimensional ideals in `Q[x, y, z]` with the local ordering `ds` given by a printed `3 x 3` matrix, from the computation of Milnor and Tjurina numbers of singularities: the Jacobian ideals of `f` (Examples 1, 2 and 4) and `<f, df/dx, df/dy, df/dz>` (Example 3). The table on p. 136 reports `modStd` (including verification) at 3, 41, 5 and 10 seconds against `std` at 258 seconds, a run stopped after 3 hours (Example 2),
  11211 seconds and 35 seconds, with `std` memory of 1.3 GB, 14 GB, 2.1 GB and 5.6 MB, computed with Singular 3-0-3 on a Linux PC with an AMD Athlon 64 2800+ at 1.8 GHz.

- [I] The lifting and verification statement of this paper is Corollary 2.3 (pp. 130-131), a Hilbert-function criterion: for `I` homogeneous, or `>` local, and `J` an ideal with `I` in `J`, `J` homogeneous when `>` is not local, and `H_(I_p) = H_J` for some prime `p`, `I = J`. Proposition 2.2 (p. 130) supplies the inequality `H_I <= H_(I_p)` that the proof uses;
  for the global homogeneous case it is the content of Theorem 5.3 and Theorem 7.1 of [Arnold 2003](arnold-2003-modular-groebner.md), to which the paper refers for the proof. The new ingredient of this paper is the local ordering, where the Hilbert-Samuel function replaces the Hilbert function and no homogeneity is assumed, stated without proof in the paper.
- [I] Comparison with the gap that the note on [Idrees, Pfister and Steidel](1005.5663.md) records for its Theorem 2.4 (the ideal `I = <2x + 1, 2y - 1>` of `Q[x, y]`, the prime `p = 2` and `G = {1}` satisfy its three hypotheses, yet `I` is proper). The statements of this paper do not admit that example. (a) For a non-local ordering the corollary requires `I` and `J` homogeneous (Proposition 2.2 and Corollary 2.3(2)); that ideal is not homogeneous,
  and Remark 2.8 states that without homogeneity the final test proves only the inclusion `<S> Q[x]_> <= <M> Q[x]_>`. (b) The criterion compares Hilbert functions, and `I_p` is the reduction of the integral part `I_0 = I intersect Z[x]` (p. 130), not of the given generators. (c) For a local ordering the same ideal gives `I Q[x]_> = Q[x]_>`, since `2x + 1` is a unit at the origin, so `G = {1}` is correct for the localization and consistent with the corollary.
  The paper therefore has no gap of that form; three limits remain. First, the proof of Proposition 2.2 is printed for neither case and is cited to Arnold for the global case only, so the local-ordering inequality is the paper's assertion. Second, in the local case the conclusion `I = J` holds for the extensions to `Q[x]_>`: for `x` one variable, a local ordering, `I = <x + 1>` and `J = <1>`, conditions (1) to (3) hold
  (all three Hilbert-Samuel functions vanish) while `I` and `J` differ in `Q[x]`; the algorithm's output is stated for `<S> Q[x]_>` (p. 134), which is the reading this note adopts. Third, the paper does not write the step from the ideal generated by the reductions of `S`, which the algorithm computes, to `I_p`. The chain
  `H_(I_p) <= H_(<S mod p>) = H_J <= H_I <= H_(I_p)` closes it when `L(M) = L(M_p)`: the first inequality holds because the reductions of `S` lie in `I_p`, the equality because `M` and `M_p` have the same leading monomials, the second inequality because `I` lies in `J`, and the last by Proposition 2.2. This chain is the repository's reading and is not printed in the paper.
- [I] Fit to the program's computations. The program works over `Q(i)` and modulo the primes 32009 and 32029, with the global ordering `dp` apart from the local ordering `ds` for one two-variable ideal ([run rulebook](../../runbooks/run-rulebook.md), job parameters). The paper states its results over `Q` and `Z`, so a transfer to `Q(i)` needs the argument over the ring of Gaussian integers that this paper does not supply
  (the paper on number fields, [1504.04564](1504.04564.md), treats that case). For a global ordering the corollary needs an ideal homogeneous in the paper's sense, and the paper mentions no weights and states no weighted-homogeneous version; whether a program ideal is homogeneous for a given grading is a property of that ideal.
  The local-ordering branch needs no grading but is a statement about the localization at the origin only, and applies to an ideal given in a ring whose ordering is local in every variable (the paper's "local ordering").
- [I] What the paper does not give. (a) No statement for mixed orderings beyond Remark 2.8. (b) No bound on the primes needed (Remark 2.7). (c) No statement for non-zero-dimensional local ideals beyond the uniqueness remark of the footnote on p. 132. (d) The timings are for four zero-dimensional examples on 2007 hardware with Singular 3-0-3, and say nothing about the program's ideals.
