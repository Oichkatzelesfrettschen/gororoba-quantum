## Construction and verification of the Lie algebra g_2 (Lecture 22, Sections 22.1 and 22.2, pp. 339-350)

- [P] Opening of Lecture 22 (p. 339): the lecture constructs `g_2` from its Dynkin diagram (Section 22.1), verifies that the result is a Lie algebra (Section 22.2), analyzes the representations of `g_2` (Section 22.3), and sketches algebraic constructions of the five exceptional Lie algebras (Section 22.4). The introduction says Section 22.3 may be read independently of Sections 22.1, 22.2 and 21.3.
- [P] Section 22.1 (pp. 339-346): the root system of the diagram `(G_2)` is two hexagons, the six roots `alpha_i` and the six roots `beta_i` (p. 340); `alpha_1` and `alpha_2` are the simple roots; the Weyl group is the dihedral group generated by a rotation through `pi/3` and a reflection (p. 340). With `X_i`, `Y_i` eigenvectors for `alpha_i`, `beta_i` and `H_i = [X_i, Y_i]`, the elements `H_1, H_2, X_1, ..., X_6, Y_1, ..., Y_6` form a basis of the 14-dimensional `g_2`, with `H_1`, `H_2` a basis of the Cartan subalgebra `h` (p. 341). The section completes the multiplication table (pp. 342-345) and then rescales the basis (Table 22.1, p. 346), after which `H_3 = H_1 + 3 H_2`, `H_4 = 2 H_1 + 3 H_2`, `H_5 = H_1 + H_2`, `H_6 = H_1 + 2 H_2` (22.2) and `[H_i, X_i] = 2 X_i`, `[H_i, Y_i] = -2 Y_i` (22.3).
- [P] Section 22.2 (pp. 346-350): the multiplication table determines the algebra, but the Jacobi identity is not yet known (p. 346). The subspace `g_0` spanned by `H_5, H_2, X_5, Y_5, X_2, Y_2, X_6, Y_6` (the Cartan subalgebra and the root spaces of the six longer roots) is isomorphic to `sl_3 C` (p. 347). With `W = C{X_4, Y_1, Y_3}` and `W* = C{Y_4, X_1, X_3}`, `g_2 = g_0 + W + W*` as a direct sum, the brackets `g_0 x W -> W` and `g_0 x W* -> W*` are the standard actions of `sl_3 C` on `C^3` and its dual, `W x W -> W*` is `v x w -> -2 v wedge w`, `W* x W* -> W` is `phi x psi -> 2 phi wedge psi`, and `W x W* -> g_0` is given by `[v, phi](w) = 3 phi(w) v - phi(v) w` (22.4) (p. 348). The Jacobi identity is verified by cases (pp. 349-350), and the section ends (p. 350) with the remark that `g_2` can be constructed directly as `sl_3 C + W + W*` with these products. The text on p. 346 says that simplicity is not much of a problem and refers to Exercise 14.34.

## Representations of g_2 (Lecture 22, Section 22.3, pp. 350-359)

- [P] Weight lattice (p. 350): with `alpha_1(H_1) = 2`, `alpha_1(H_2) = -1`, `alpha_2(H_1) = -3`, `alpha_2(H_2) = 2`, the weight lattice is generated by `alpha_1` and `alpha_2` and equals the root lattice. Fundamental weights (p. 351): `omega_1 = 2 alpha_1 + alpha_2` and `omega_2 = 3 alpha_1 + 2 alpha_2`; the intersection of the closed Weyl chamber with the weight lattice is the free semigroup on them, and `Gamma_(a,b)` denotes the irreducible representation of highest weight `a omega_1 + b omega_2`.
- [P] Smallest representations (p. 352): `Gamma_(1,0)` has dimension 7 (the weight 0 has multiplicity 1), is the smallest irreducible representation of `g_2`, and is called the standard representation `V`; `Gamma_(0,1)` is the adjoint representation, of dimension 14, in which the weight 0 has multiplicity 2. Decomposition of the exterior square (p. 353): `Lambda^2 V` is isomorphic to `Gamma_(0,1)` plus `V`; the book concludes that every irreducible representation of `g_2` appears in some tensor power `V^(tensor m)`, since `Gamma_(a,b)` lies in `Sym^a V tensor Sym^b Gamma_(0,1)`.
- [P] Symmetric square and the invariant quadratic form (pp. 354-356): from an explicit basis `v_4, v_3, v_1, u, w_1, w_3, w_4` of `V` (p. 354; Exercises 22.9 and 22.10 ask for the checks), direct computation of the weight multiplicities shows `Sym^2 V` is isomorphic to `Gamma_(2,0)` plus the trivial representation `C` (p. 355); the book deduces that the action of `g_2` on `V = C^7` preserves a quadratic form, so `g_2` contained in `sl(V)` lies in `so_7 C` (p. 356).
- [P] Exterior cube and the invariant cubic form (pp. 356-357): `Lambda^3 V` is isomorphic to `Gamma_(2,0)` plus `V` plus `C`, so the action of `g_2` on `V` preserves a skew-symmetric trilinear form `omega` on `V`, written explicitly on p. 357 in the basis above. The dimension count on p. 357: `Lambda^3 V` has dimension 35, `gl(V)` has dimension 49, and the difference is 14, the dimension of `g_2`; the text states that the linear map `phi : gl(V) -> Lambda^3 V` sending `A` to `A(omega)` can be checked directly to be surjective and concludes that `omega` is a general cubic alternating form (an open dense subset of `Lambda^3 V` corresponds to forms equivalent to `omega` under `Aut(V)`).
- [P] Proposition 22.12 (p. 357): `g_2` is exactly the algebra of endomorphisms of a seven-dimensional vector space `V` preserving a general skew-symmetric cubic form `omega` on `V`. The surjectivity of `phi` on which the proof rests is assigned to the reader in Exercise 22.13* (p. 358, starred, stated without proof); Exercise 22.14 (p. 358, stated without proof) asks the reader to show that on `C^n` with `n <= 7` there are only finitely many skew-symmetric trilinear forms up to the action of `GL_n C` and to verify that `omega` is general in `Lambda^3 C^7`; a parenthesis adds that the same finiteness holds on `C^8`, though the proof is complicated, and that for `n >= 9` a dimension count gives a continuously varying family.
- [P] Pages 358-359: identifying `g_2 = g_0 + W + W*` with `sl_3 C + W + W*`, the standard representation is `V = W + W* + C` as a representation of `sl_3 C`; the book tabulates how `g_2` acts on `V` (p. 358) and writes `omega = sum for i = 1..3 of e_i wedge u wedge e_i* + 2 (e_1 wedge e_2 wedge e_3 + e_1* wedge e_2* wedge e_3*)` (p. 359) with `u = 1` in `C`.

## Algebraic constructions and the octonions (Lecture 22, Section 22.4, pp. 359-365)

- [P] Proposition 22.19 (Freudenthal, p. 361): given a representation `W` of a semisimple Lie algebra `g_0` and trilinear forms `T`, `T'` inducing maps `Lambda^2 W -> W*` and `Lambda^2 W* -> W` that satisfy the identities (22.17) and (22.18) of p. 360, the products of p. 360 make `g = g_0 + W + W*` into a Lie algebra; if the weight spaces of `W` are one dimensional, the weights of `W` and `W*` and the roots of `g_0` are all distinct, and the scalars `a`, `b`, `c` of the brackets on p. 360 satisfy `a b c != 0`, then `g` is semisimple with the same Cartan subalgebra as `g_0`. The book applies it to `g_0 = sl_9 C` and `W = Lambda^3 C^9` for `e_8` (dimension `80 + 84 + 84 = 248`, p. 361), leaving the verifications to Exercise 22.21*.
- [P] Pages 362-363: the text states that the exceptional group `G_2` can be realized as the group of automorphisms of the complexification of the eight-dimensional Cayley algebra (the octonions) `O`, and that the exceptional Lie algebras can be realized as Lie algebras of derivations of nonassociative algebras (p. 362). `O` is built as the set of pairs `(a, b)` of quaternions with componentwise addition and the product `(a, b) o (c, d) = (a c - conj(d) b, d a + b conj(c))`; its conjugation takes `(a, b)` to `(conj(a), -b)`; a basis is `1 = (1, 0)` with `e_1, ..., e_7 = (i, 0), (j, 0), (k, 0), (0, 1), (0, i), (0, j), (0, k)`, with `e_p o e_p = -1` and `e_p o e_q = - e_q o e_p` for `p != q`, and the multiplication table is encoded by the diagram of p. 363 (a triangle with seven points and arrowed lines). A page-363 sentence says that the next exercise sketches a proof that the complexification of `G = Aut(O)`, the group of algebra automorphisms of the real algebra `O`, is a Lie group of type `(G_2)`.
- [P] Exercise 22.26 (p. 363, stated without proof): the subalgebra generated by any two elements of `O` is isomorphic to `R`, `C` or `H`, so `O` is alternative. Trace `Tr(x) = (x + conj(x))/2` and norm `N(x) = x o conj(x)` are defined on `O`, with an associated bilinear form `beta` for which the basis above is orthonormal (p. 363).
- [P] Exercise 22.27* (p. 364, starred and stated without proof; the hints beginning at p. 516 were not read): for `G = Aut(O)` the real automorphism group, with `Y` the orthogonal complement of `R.1` for `N`, so that `G` embeds in `SO(Y)`: (a) the "cross product" `v x w = v o w + beta(v, w) 1` on `Y` identifies `G` with the group of orthogonal transformations of `Y` preserving it; (b) `G` acts transitively on the 6-sphere `S^6` of unit vectors of `Y`, and the subgroup `K` fixing `i = e_1` is mapped onto the 5-sphere in the orthogonal complement of `e_1` by `g -> g.j`, from which the exercise concludes that `G` is 14-dimensional and simply connected; (c) the algebra `{D in Der(O) : D(i) = 0}` is isomorphic to `su_3`; (d) the Lie algebra of derivations of the complex octonions is the simple Lie algebra of type `(G_2)`. Exercises 22.28 and 22.29 (pp. 364-365) recast the octonions through the Clifford algebra and triality. Page 365 describes `f_4` as the derivation algebra of the complexification of a 27-dimensional Jordan algebra and states the cubic form whose automorphism group is of type `(E_6)`, without proof.

## Homogeneous spaces, closed orbits and the G_2 examples (Lecture 23, Section 23.3, pp. 382-394)

- [P] Borel subalgebras and subgroups (pp. 382-383): for a semisimple Lie algebra `g` with Cartan subalgebra `h` and an ordering `R = R+ union R-` of the roots, `b = h + (direct sum of g_alpha for alpha in R+)` is a Borel subalgebra (solvable, and maximal solvable by Exercise 14.35); for a Lie group `G` with semisimple Lie algebra `g`, the connected subgroup `B` with Lie algebra `b` is a Borel subgroup. Claim 23.45 (p. 383): `B` is a closed subgroup of `G` and the quotient `G/B` is compact. The page lists the classical cases: the flag manifold for `SL_(n+1) C` and varieties of isotropic flags for the orthogonal and symplectic groups.
- [P] Parabolic subgroups (pp. 384-385): a (closed, complex analytic, connected) subgroup `P` of `G` is called parabolic if `G/P` can be realized as the orbit of `G` on `P(V)` for some representation `V` of `G`; every Borel subgroup is parabolic. Claim 23.47: for a Borel subgroup `B` and a parabolic subgroup `P` there is `x` in `G` with `B` contained in `x P x^-1`; Claim 23.48: a subgroup `P` of `G` that contains `B` is parabolic. The deduction of Claim 23.47 uses Borel's fixed point theorem (p. 384): a connected solvable group acting on a projective variety in `P(V)` that it preserves has a fixed point on it. The classification (pp. 384-385): up to conjugacy, the parabolic subgroups of a simple group `G` are in one-to-one correspondence with subsets of the nodes of its Dynkin diagram, through the subalgebras `p(Sigma) = h + (direct sum of g_alpha for alpha in T(Sigma))` of (23.49), where `T(Sigma)` consists of the positive roots and the roots that are sums of negatives of roots in `Sigma`.
- [P] Claim 23.52 (p. 388): let `G` be a simple group (as the text of p. 388 sets the case), `V = Gamma_lambda` the irreducible representation with highest weight `lambda`, and `p` in `P(V)` the point of the weight space for `lambda`; then the orbit `G.p` is the unique closed orbit of `G` on `P(V)`. The proof: `p` is fixed by the Borel subgroup `B`, so its stabilizer is parabolic and the orbit `G/P_lambda` is compact, hence closed; conversely, by Borel's fixed point theorem any closed orbit contains a point fixed by `B`, and `p` is the unique such point of `P(V)`. The text then says `P_lambda` is the parabolic subgroup attached to the simple roots perpendicular to `lambda`, and (pp. 388-389) matches faces of the Weyl chamber with parabolic subgroups, so that weights in the interior give the full flag manifold `G/B` and weights on the edges give quotients by maximal parabolic subgroups.
- [P] Examples (pp. 389-390): for `SL_3 C`, the representations `Sym^k V` and `Sym^k V*` have closed orbit `P^2`, and the adjoint representation has as closed orbit the variety of traceless rank-one homomorphisms, isomorphic to the flag manifold by `phi -> (Im phi, Ker phi)` (a hyperplane section of `P(V) x P(V*)` in `P^8`); for `Sp_4 C` the closed orbit in `P(W)`, `W` the complement of the trivial representation in `Lambda^2 V`, is the variety of isotropic 2-planes, which the diagram of p. 390 labels a quadric hypersurface in `P(W)`; the spin representations of `Spin_(2n+1) C` and `Spin_(2n) C` have the orthogonal Grassmannians of isotropic subspaces as closed orbits (the spinor varieties).
- [P] The compact homogeneous spaces of `G_2` (pp. 390-392): `G_2` has two maximal parabolic subgroups, each of dimension 9, so both homogeneous spaces are 5-dimensional (p. 391). For the standard 7-dimensional representation `V`, the closed orbit in `P(V) = P^6` is a homogeneous hypersurface, which the text says can only be a quadric; the book reads this as a second proof that `G_2` preserves a nondegenerate bilinear form, `G_2` contained in `SO_7 C` (p. 391). The other homogeneous space `Y` is described through the adjoint representation `W` contained in `Lambda^2 V`: `Y` lies in the intersection of the Grassmannian `G(1, 7)` of lines in `P(V)` with `P(W)` inside `P(Lambda^2 V)`, hence in the locus `Sigma = {Lambda contained in V : omega(Lambda, Lambda, .) = 0}` in `G(2, V)` for the invariant cubic form `omega` (p. 392). Problem 23.54 (p. 392) asks whether `Y = Sigma`; the book states it as an open problem. Page 392 then describes the correspondence between irreducible representations and line bundles on `G/P` and, with Claims 23.56 and 23.57 (p. 393), Bott's theorem for line bundles `L_lambda` on `G/B`; Diagram 23.58 (p. 394) collects the correspondences between Dynkin-diagram subsets, Weyl-chamber faces, parabolic subgroups, flag manifolds and irreducible representations.
