## Result and reading scope

[P] Theorem 2 on PDF page 4 gives sufficient conditions for efficient classical
simulation. The input is a product of squeezed displaced vacuum states. The
allowed dynamics are generated by inhomogeneous quadratic Hamiltonians in the
canonical position and momentum operators. The measurements are canonical
quadrature measurements, including finite linear losses. Classical feedforward
may select Gaussian Clifford operations from classical numbers or measured
quadratures. The earlier restriction on feedforward in the proof is explicitly
relaxed on pages 3-4; a summary restricted only to displacement feedforward would
understate the final theorem.

[P] Pages 2-3 identify the continuous-variable Clifford group with phase-space
translations and symplectic transformations. Pages 3-4 explain simulation by
tracking linear transformations of the canonical generators and sampling
Gaussian variables. Entanglement between the oscillators is permitted. Page 4
also addresses finite precision: the discretization error must remain bounded,
with precision related to squeezing and detector resolution; the paper argues
polynomial overhead for that control.

[P] Photon counting is outside the stated measurement family. Page 4 discusses
ideal photodetection as a route to measurement-induced nonlinearity. Higher
than quadratic Hamiltonians also leave the sufficient simulation theorem.
The source does not claim that every task outside its hypotheses is hard.

Reading scope: the five-page PDF, with detailed attention to the definitions,
proof argument, Theorem 2, numerical-precision paragraph, and measurement
restrictions on pages 2-4. The universal-computation references cited by the
paper were not independently audited during this task.

## Consequences for experiment design

[I] An oscillator experiment must name its output task before a resource
comparison: a few means and covariances, quadrature samples, photon counts,
state reconstruction, and process reconstruction require different references.
The Gaussian mean/covariance simulator is a relevant baseline for the first two
within the theorem's hypotheses. The theorem alone does not certify an
efficient sampler for arbitrary photon-count distributions.

[I] A finite Fock cutoff changes the algebra at the boundary. For the usual
truncated ladder matrices on levels `0,...,d-1`,

```text
[a_d, a_d^dagger] = I_d - d |d-1><d-1|.
```

The identity follows by comparing the diagonal entries of the two products.
It supplies a concrete boundary diagnostic rather than an assumption that the
finite register obeys the infinite canonical commutation relation. A qubit
encoding can therefore introduce non-Clifford gates while representing a
small, classically tractable truncated problem. Neither the cutoff nor the
qubit gate alphabet establishes sampling hardness.

[I] Compare two references separately: an exact or certified finite-dimensional
simulator for the circuit's encoded target, and the infinite-dimensional
Gaussian prediction for the physical model. Vary the cutoff and track
population at the highest retained level. A convergence claim additionally
needs a quantitative tail or truncation bound. Agreement at one cutoff is a
finite validation result.

## Candidate proof obligations

1. Prove the finite ladder commutator with explicit dimension and matrix-index
   conventions. Use an exact coefficient field or justified square-root
   identities; floating matrices do not supply a formal certificate.
2. Prove the encoding map, observable map, and ideal circuit semantics for each
   finite target. Retain global phase when a block is coherently controlled.
3. Prove the relation between measured probabilities and the requested
   quadrature moments. Record basis rotations and units as premises.
4. If an infinite-oscillator claim is needed, prove a truncation error bound
   under stated energy/tail hypotheses and combine it with synthesis and
   statistical errors. A finite-matrix proof does not discharge that premise.
5. Treat simulator runtime and sampling complexity as a separate algorithmic
   result with precision, parameter-size, and output-task costs named.

[I] A useful falsifier for a proposed Gaussian interpretation is a measured
observable or outcome law that contradicts the model after the admitted
instrument and statistical errors. Failure of the ideal Gaussian reference
by itself leaves truncation, compilation, preparation, measurement, and
hardware noise as competing explanations.
