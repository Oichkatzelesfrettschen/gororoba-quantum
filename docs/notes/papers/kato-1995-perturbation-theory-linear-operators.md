## The eigenvalue problem, resolvent and eigenprojections (Chapter One, Section 5, pp. 34-43)

- [P] Setting (p. 34): `X` is a vector space with `0 < dim X = N < infinity`, normed when convenient; `T` is a linear operator on `X`. An eigenvalue is a complex `lambda` with `T u = lambda u` for some `u != 0` (5.1, p. 35); the spectrum `Sigma(T)` has at most `N` points.
- [P] Resolvent (p. 36): `R(zeta) = R(zeta, T) = (T - zeta)^-1` for `zeta` in the resolvent set `P(T)`, the complement of `Sigma(T)`. The book states in a footnote that it follows Stone's convention, `(T - zeta)^-1` rather than `(zeta - T)^-1`, so the eigenprojection integrals below carry a minus sign. The resolvent equation is `R(zeta_1) - R(zeta_2) = (zeta_1 - zeta_2) R(zeta_1) R(zeta_2)` (5.5, p. 36); the first Neumann series (5.6, p. 37) converges for `|zeta - zeta_0| < ||R(zeta_0)||^-1`.
- [P] Singularities (pp. 38-40): at an eigenvalue `lambda_h` the Laurent series of `R(zeta)` is `R(zeta) = -(zeta - lambda_h)^-1 P_h - sum for n >= 1 of (zeta - lambda_h)^(-n-1) D_h^n + sum for n >= 0 of (zeta - lambda_h)^n S_h^(n+1)` (5.18, p. 39), where `P_h` is a projection (the eigenprojection), `D_h` is nilpotent (the eigennilpotent) with `D_h^(m_h) = 0` and `m_h = dim P_h X` (5.20), and `S_h` is the reduced resolvent; so `R` has a pole of order at most `m_h` at `lambda_h` (p. 39). The eigenprojections satisfy `P_h P_k = delta_hk P_h`, `sum of P_h = 1`, `P_h T = T P_h` (5.21), and `P_h = -(1/(2 pi i)) times the integral of R(zeta) d zeta over a circle Gamma_h` enclosing `lambda_h` and no other eigenvalue (5.22, p. 39). Partial fractions (5.23, p. 40): `R(zeta) = -sum over h of [ (zeta - lambda_h)^-1 P_h + sum for n = 1..m_h-1 of (zeta - lambda_h)^(-n-1) D_h^n ]`. Problem 5.9 (p. 40, a problem): for a simple closed rectifiable curve `Gamma` with positive direction that passes through no eigenvalue, `(1/(2 pi i)) times the integral of R(zeta) d zeta over Gamma` equals `- sum of P_h` over the eigenvalues inside `Gamma`.
- [P] Canonical form (pp. 40-43): `M_h = P_h X` is the algebraic eigenspace of `lambda_h` and `m_h` its algebraic multiplicity; `X = M_1 + ... + M_s` as a direct sum (5.34); `T P_h = P_h T = P_h T P_h = lambda_h P_h + D_h` (5.35). `T = S + D` with `S = sum of lambda_h P_h` diagonalizable and `D = sum of D_h` nilpotent commuting with `S` (5.36 to 5.38); this spectral representation is unique (pp. 41-42). An eigenvalue is semisimple if `D_h = 0` and simple if `m_h = 1` (p. 41). Formula (5.40, p. 42): `det(T - zeta) = product of (lambda_h - zeta)^(m_h)` and `tr T = sum of m_h lambda_h`. Problem 5.12 (p. 43, a problem): the geometric eigenspace `N_h` lies in `M_h`, with equality if and only if `lambda_h` is semisimple; Problem 5.16 (p. 43, a problem): the eigenvalues are the roots of `det(T - zeta) = 0` with multiplicity equal to the algebraic multiplicity.

## Analytic perturbation of eigenvalues (Chapter Two, Section 1, pp. 63-74)

- [P] Setting (p. 63; footnote 1 there): `0 < dim X = N < infinity`. The family is `T(kappa) = T + kappa T'` (1.1), more generally `T(kappa) = T + kappa T^(1) + kappa^2 T^(2) + ...` (1.2), or any operator-valued function `T(kappa)` holomorphic in a domain `D_0` of the `kappa`-plane; `T(0) = T` is the unperturbed operator. The eigenvalues are the roots of `det(T(kappa) - zeta) = 0` (1.3), an algebraic equation of degree `N` with coefficients holomorphic in `kappa` (p. 63).
- [P] Algebraic singularities (p. 64): by "a well-known result in function theory", cited to Knopp ([2], p. 119) and not proved in the book, the roots of (1.3) form one or several analytic functions of `kappa` with only algebraic singularities in `D_0`. Hence the number `s` of distinct eigenvalues of `T(kappa)` is constant except at exceptional points, which are finite in number in each compact subset of `D_0`; `s = N` if these analytic functions are all distinct (then `T(kappa)` is simple, hence diagonalizable, at the non-exceptional points), and `s < N` means `T(kappa)` is permanently degenerate. Example 1.1 (p. 64) lists six `2 x 2` families, among them (a) `T(kappa) = [[1, kappa], [kappa, -1]]` with eigenvalues `+-(1 + kappa^2)^(1/2)`, exceptional points `kappa = +-i`, and `T(+-i)` having the single eigenvalue 0; and (d) `T(kappa) = [[0, 1], [kappa, 0]]` with eigenvalues `+-kappa^(1/2)` and exceptional point `kappa = 0`.
- [P] Puiseux series (p. 65, Section 1.2): on a simple subdomain `D` of `D_0` (a simply connected subdomain without exceptional points) the eigenvalues are `s` holomorphic functions `lambda_1(kappa), ..., lambda_s(kappa)` (1.5). Near an exceptional point (taken to be `kappa = 0`) they group into cycles of period `p`, each a branch of one analytic function with Puiseux series `lambda_h(kappa) = lambda + alpha_1 omega^h kappa^(1/p) + alpha_2 omega^(2h) kappa^(2/p) + ...`, `omega = exp(2 pi i/p)`, `h = 0, ..., p - 1` (1.7), with no negative powers because the coefficient of the highest power `zeta^N` in (1.3) is `(-1)^N`; `lambda` is the center of the cycle. The page states that `|lambda_h(kappa) - lambda|` is "in general" of the order `|kappa|^(1/p)` for small `|kappa|`, so for `p >= 2` the rate of change at an exceptional point is infinitely large compared with the change of `T(kappa)`. The Puiseux form is cited to Knopp ([2], p. 130) and the continuity at the exceptional point to Knopp ([2], p. 122). Problem 1.2 (p. 66, a problem): the sum of the `lambda_h(kappa)` of one cycle is holomorphic at the exceptional point. The eigenvalues of all cycles with center `lambda` are said to depart from `lambda` by splitting at `kappa = 0` and form the `lambda`-group (p. 66). Remark 1.3 (p. 66): an exceptional point need not be a branch point; two different eigenvalues for `kappa != kappa_0` must coincide at an exceptional point `kappa_0`, so there is splitting at, and only at, an exceptional point.
- [P] Theorem 1.5 (p. 66): the resolvent `R(zeta, kappa) = (T(kappa) - zeta)^-1` is holomorphic in the two variables `zeta` and `kappa` in each domain in which `zeta` is not equal to any eigenvalue of `T(kappa)`. The second Neumann series (1.13, p. 67) expands `R(zeta, kappa)` in powers of `kappa` with coefficients `R^(n)(zeta)` given by (1.14), converging uniformly for small `|kappa|` and `zeta` in a compact subset of the resolvent set of `T`.
- [P] Total projection (pp. 67-68): for an eigenvalue `lambda` of `T = T(0)` with algebraic multiplicity `m` and a closed positively oriented curve `Gamma` (say a circle) in the resolvent set of `T` that encloses `lambda` and no other eigenvalue of `T`, `P(kappa) = -(1/(2 pi i)) times the integral of R(zeta, kappa) d zeta over Gamma` (1.16) is, for `|kappa|` small, a projection equal to the sum of the eigenprojections of the eigenvalues of `T(kappa)` inside `Gamma` (by Problem I-5.9); `P(kappa) = P + sum for n >= 1 of kappa^n P^(n)` with `P^(n) = -(1/(2 pi i)) times the integral of R^(n)(zeta) d zeta` (1.17, 1.18) is holomorphic near `kappa = 0`, `P(0)` is the eigenprojection of `lambda`, and `dim P(kappa) = dim P = m` (1.19). The eigenvalues inside `Gamma` form exactly the `lambda`-group; `P(kappa)` is called the total projection and `M(kappa) = P(kappa) X` the total eigenspace. On a simple subdomain `D` each eigenprojection `P_h(kappa)` and each eigennilpotent `D_h(kappa) = (T(kappa) - lambda_h(kappa)) P_h(kappa)` is holomorphic, and the multiplicities `m_h` are constant (p. 68, formulas 1.20 and 1.21).
- [P] Theorem 1.8 (p. 70): the eigenvalues `lambda_h(kappa)`, the eigenprojections `P_h(kappa)` and the eigennilpotents `D_h(kappa)` of `T(kappa)` are (branches of) analytic functions for `kappa` in `D_0` with only algebraic singularities at some, not necessarily all, exceptional points; `lambda_h` and `P_h` have all their branch points in common, while `D_h` may or may not have branch points there; in particular, if `lambda_h(kappa)` is single-valued near an exceptional point (a cycle of period 1), so are `P_h` and `D_h`. Theorem 1.9 (p. 70, due to Butler per the footnote): if `kappa_0` is a branch point of `lambda_h(kappa)`, and hence of `P_h(kappa)`, of order `p - 1 >= 1`, then `P_h(kappa)` has a pole there, its Laurent expansion in powers of `(kappa - kappa_0)^(1/p)` has negative powers, and `||P_h(kappa)||` tends to infinity as `kappa` tends to `kappa_0`. Page 71: an eigenvalue of `T` of multiplicity `m` does not give rise to a branch point of order larger than `m - 1`.
- [P] Theorem 1.10 (p. 71): let `X` be a unitary space, `kappa_0` in `D_0` (possibly an exceptional point), and let there be a sequence `kappa_n` converging to `kappa_0` such that `T(kappa_n)` is normal for `n = 1, 2, ...`; then all `lambda_h(kappa)` and `P_h(kappa)` are holomorphic at `kappa = kappa_0` and `D_h(kappa)` vanishes identically. Remark 1.11 and Example 1.12 (pp. 71-72) record that the `P_h` and `D_h` need not be defined at an exceptional point, and compute them for the six families of Example 1.1; in family (d) the eigenprojections have a branch point at `kappa = 0`.
- [P] Linear dependence (pp. 72-73, Section 1.7): for `T(kappa) = T + kappa T'` the domain `D_0` is the whole plane, the coefficients of (1.3) are polynomials in `kappa` of degree at most `N`, the `lambda_h(kappa)` are branches of algebraic functions with no pole at a finite `kappa`, and at `kappa = infinity` they have at most a pole of order 1, with expansion `lambda_h(kappa) = mu_h kappa + beta_h kappa^(1 - 1/p) + ...` (1.34); footnote 1 on p. 73 says the results also hold when `T(kappa)` is a polynomial in `kappa` of any degree. Section 1.8 (pp. 73-74) summarizes the section.

## Perturbation series (Chapter Two, Section 2, pp. 74-78)

- [P] Setting (p. 74): `T(kappa) = T + kappa T^(1) + kappa^2 T^(2) + ...` (2.1) with `0 < dim X = N < infinity` (footnote on p. 74); `lambda` is an eigenvalue of `T` of algebraic multiplicity `m`, with eigenprojection `P` and eigennilpotent `D`, so `T P = P T = P T P = lambda P + D`, `dim P = m`, `D^m = 0`, `P D = D P = D` (2.2). The section constructs the Taylor series of the `lambda`-group functions formally; convergence radii and error estimates are deferred to later sections (p. 74), which were not read.
- [P] Pages 75-77: `P(kappa) = sum of kappa^n P^(n)` with `P^(0) = P` (2.3); the `lambda`-group eigenvalues of `T(kappa)` are the nonzero eigenvalues of `T_r(kappa) = T(kappa) P(kappa) = P(kappa) T(kappa) P(kappa)` (2.4), provided `|lambda|` is large enough that they do not vanish for small `|kappa|`; the weighted mean of the `lambda`-group eigenvalues is `lambda-hat(kappa) = (1/m) tr(T(kappa) P(kappa)) = lambda + (1/m) tr((T(kappa) - lambda) P(kappa))` (2.5), equal to `lambda(kappa)` if there is no splitting (2.6). With `S` the value of the reduced resolvent at `zeta = lambda` and the Laurent expansion (2.9) of `R(zeta)`, the coefficients are `P^(n) = -sum for p = 1..n of (-1)^p sum of S^(k_1) T^(nu_1) S^(k_2) ... T^(nu_p) S^(k_(p+1))` over `nu_1 + ... + nu_p = n`, `k_1 + ... + k_(p+1) = p`, `nu_j >= 1`, `k_j >= -m + 1` (2.12, p. 76); for a semisimple eigenvalue (`D = 0`) only nonnegative `k_j` contribute, and `P^(1) = -P T^(1) S - S T^(1) P` (2.14, p. 77).
- [P] Pages 77-78: the series of the weighted mean is `lambda-hat(kappa) = lambda + sum for n >= 1 of kappa^n lambda-hat^(n)` with `lambda-hat^(n) = (1/m) tr T-tilde^(n)` (2.21, 2.22), where `T-tilde^(n)` is built like `P^(n)` with the extra factor `(zeta - lambda)` in the integrand (2.17, 2.18); an alternative expression is the logarithmic contour formula (2.23, p. 78).
